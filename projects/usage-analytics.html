Turning raw platform clickstreams into the actual business processes people run on o9, without asking anyone to self-report what they did.

Problem

Every click on the o9 platform, every filter applied, every action button fired, already gets logged. What was missing was a way to turn that raw event stream into an answer to a much more useful question: what is this team actually doing all month, and where is the effort going?

Why it matters

Teams could describe their process in a meeting. Nobody could show it. This makes the actual, as-executed process visible from the data itself.

Users

Team leads who need to know where their team's time actually goes are the primary audience, alongside implementation teams checking whether a delivered solution is genuinely being used or quietly abandoned after go-live. Individual platform users are the indirect beneficiaries: their real work becomes visible automatically, without filling out a status report about it.

Why existing approaches failed

o9 platform usage is logged at a fine grain: every viewA saved combination of metrics and categories presented to a user. opened, every reportA specific chart or table surfaced inside a view. looked at, every action buttonA UI trigger that runs a plugin or rule against the platform. fired. That log already exists. What doesn't exist by default is a way to read it as a process rather than a list of isolated events. The only alternative was asking a team to describe its own workflow in a meeting, which answers what people believe they do, not what the data says they actually do.

Product insight

Frequency, not existence, is what defines a real process. Any single click sequence could be a one-off experiment. A sequence a person runs twenty times in a month is a workflow. That reframing meant the system didn't need anyone to label or categorize their own behavior, it just needed to count how often each path repeated and let the highest-frequency paths speak for themselves.

System approach

Architecture — per-user daily graphs merge into one aggregate monthly graph, and that aggregate graph is what surfaces business-process insight: through an interactive diagram, four report views, and the agent's plain-English monthly summary.

Clicks into daily graphs

For every user and every day, the system reconstructs the sequence of views, reports, and action buttons touched, in order, as a small directed graph: a Start node, the sequence of platform entities visited, and an End node. Where an action button fired, the specific data it acted on travels along with it, so the graph carries not just "an action ran" but what it actually acted on.

Daily into monthly, with frequency

Daily graphs for the same user are merged into one monthly graph where repeated paths accumulate a frequency count instead of being listed as separate events. The structure stays the same, Start through a sequence of steps to End, but now every edge carries a number: how many times that step happened that month. The paths with the highest frequency are, by definition, the process this person actually runs regularly.

An interactive diagram, and four report views

A simplified version of that monthly graph is rendered as an interactive diagram: hover a node for what it represents, hover an edge for how often that step happened, click an action node for the data breakdown that traveled with it.

The underlying monthly graphs across a team feed four distinct report views, since a team lead, an adoption auditor, and someone spotting month-end crunches are each asking a different shape of question:

Executive summary

The team's highest-frequency paths across the month, plus individual highlights for anyone whose activity stands out from the group.

Members worked on

Which specific items and locations were touched by which activities, and by whom, broken out by dimension.

Activities worked on

A ranked view of the platform's most-run activities that month, both team-wide and per person.

Work hotspots

A day-by-day view of which activities spiked on which days, useful for spotting month-end crunches or recurring cycles.

Key PM decisions

  • Built per-user, per-day graphs first, not one giant graph. Keeping each unit of computation small and cheap is what makes daily reconstruction viable at all, and it keeps any single day's trace easy to inspect and debug on its own.
  • Chose frequency-weighted paths over a simple ranked feature list. A ranked list of "most clicked" would lose the sequence, what led to what. Frequency on a graph preserves order as well as popularity, which is what actually distinguishes a workflow from a pile of popular clicks.
  • Split into four report views instead of one dashboard. A team lead, someone auditing adoption, and someone hunting month-end crunches are asking different questions, so each gets a view shaped for its own question instead of one screen trying to answer all three at once.

Human-in-the-loop & trust

Reconstructing behavior automatically, with nobody self-reporting anything, raises an obvious trust question: how does a team lead know the system got it right? The answer was to keep every level inspectable rather than asking anyone to trust a single opaque score. A summary number always traces back to the report view behind it, which traces back to the interactive monthly graph, which traces back to the daily graphs it was built from, so a skeptical reader can always click down to the raw path that produced any given claim.

Tradeoffs & risks

Frequency captures what happened and how often, not why, so interpreting a spike or a new high-frequency path still needs a human who knows the business context, the system surfaces the pattern, not the explanation. A single unusual month, a crunch period or a one-time migration, can also distort what looks like "the real process" if read in isolation; the day-by-day hotspot view exists specifically to catch that kind of anomaly rather than assuming a month is always representative.

Impact

Beyond the reporting views, the same monthly graphs are the input to an agent that turns the aggregated metrics into a plain-English monthly summary for a team lead, replacing a report a team lead used to have to assemble by hand every month. The same process-mining approach also gives implementation teams an honest, data-backed way to see which parts of a solution are actually being used, versus configured and forgotten, turning "is this adopted?" from a guess into something they can check directly.
